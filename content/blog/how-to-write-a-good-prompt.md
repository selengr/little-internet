## The 4 C's of a good prompt

A good prompt usually does four things.

**Clarity. Context. Constraints. Conversation.**

- **Clarity** — say exactly what you want.
- **Context** — give the AI what it needs to know.
- **Constraints** — set the limits: format, length, style, tone.
- **Conversation** — check the result, then refine it.

Here's a simple example.

A vague prompt:

> "Make my CV better."

A clearer one:

> "Rewrite my CV summary for a frontend developer role. Keep it under 80 words, professional but natural, and focus on my React and Next.js experience."

Notice what changed. The second prompt isn't longer just to be longer — it's complete enough for the task.

**A good prompt doesn't have to be long. It has to be complete enough for the task.**

## Don't try to sound smart

One of the most common mistakes is trying to sound technical.

AI doesn't need fancy words. It needs clear instructions.

Instead of:

> "Please provide an aesthetically pleasing and highly optimized professional biography."

Just say:

> "Write a short professional bio for my portfolio. Keep it natural and confident."

**Simple beats fancy. Every time.**

## Give AI what it can't know

AI only knows the context you give it.

If your goal depends on your experience, your audience, your budget, your preferences, your existing code, or your past work — say so. Otherwise, it has to fill in the gaps itself — and it may make assumptions you didn't intend.

> "Fix this React component. Keep the existing behavior and TypeScript types. Explain only the changes that are necessary."

Now the AI has something real to work with — not just "fix my code."

## Set useful constraints

Constraints aren't restrictions. They're instructions.

Telling AI what to avoid can help. Telling it what a good result actually looks like helps more.

Instead of:

> "Don't make it too long. Don't make it boring. Don't overcomplicate it."

Try:

> "Keep it under 150 words, casual tone, and easy to skim."

Give it something to walk toward — not just a list of things to dodge.

## Your first prompt doesn't need to be perfect

Think of AI as a conversation, not a vending machine. You ask, you get an answer, you see what's missing, and you adjust.

> "Explain closures in JavaScript as if I already understand functions but have never learned closures. Use one simple example."

Then:

> "Good — now show the same idea using a real-world analogy instead of code."

Then:

> "Perfect. Now write that as a short paragraph I can save in my notes."

That's a conversation. And a conversation almost always beats one giant, perfect prompt.

## Different AI models behave differently

Different models are trained and designed in different ways. They can have different strengths, context limits, reasoning abilities, and ways of following instructions. Because of that, the same prompt can produce different results depending on which model you use.

You don't need to learn a different prompting language for every model. Learn the fundamentals — explain your goal, provide the important context, set useful constraints, and refine the result — and they carry over everywhere.

## So, what is an LLM?

You've probably heard the term **LLM** — short for *Large Language Model*.

You don't need to understand the math behind one to use it well.

In simple terms: an LLM is a model trained on large amounts of data to learn patterns in language and other information. When you give it a prompt, it uses those learned patterns, along with the context you provide, to generate a response.

That's why your instructions and context matter so much.

But remember one thing:

**AI doesn't know what you meant. It only has what you gave it.**
